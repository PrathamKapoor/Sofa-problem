# The Moving Sofa Problem — complete solution

**TL;DR.** The sofa of maximal area that can be moved around a right-angled
corner in a unit-width hallway is **Gerver's sofa** (Joseph L. Gerver, 1992),
whose area is

> **2.21953166887196...**  (OEIS A128463)

Its optimality was proved in 2024 by **Jineon Baek** ("Optimality of
Gerver's Sofa", arXiv:2411.19826). In this folder the sofa is reconstructed
**exactly** from first principles (Romik's ODE theory), its area is verified
**five independent ways** (agreement to ~1e−14), the construction is
cross-checked against every published constant, and the famous "0.0012
clearance" of the passage is reproduced numerically.

---

## 1. The problem

> **The moving sofa problem** (Moser, "Problem 66-11", SIAM Review 8(3):381,
> 1966). What is the planar shape of largest area that can be moved
> continuously around a right-angled corner in a hallway of unit width?

The hallway is the "L" formed by two unit-wide corridors. A "sofa" is any
connected planar set S that can be rigidly transported — translations and
rotations only, no deforming — from the far end of one corridor, through the
90° turn, into the other corridor. The answer is a single number, the
**sofa constant**

    μ_MS  =  sup { area(S) : S can pass the corner }.

Leo Moser posed it in 1966 ("loading a sofa into a moving van", Problem G1
in Croft–Falconer–Guy, *Unsolved Problems in Geometry*). For 58 years the
best candidate was Gerver's construction; in November 2024 Jineon Baek
posted a 119-page proof that it is optimal, closing the problem.

## 3. Setup: hallway copies and the rotation path

Fix width 1 and use the sofa's own frame (conventions of Romik,
arXiv:1606.08111, §2). The two corridors are the strips

    L_horiz = {(x,y): x ≤ 1, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1},     L_vert = {(x,y): 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, y ≤ 1},

and the hallway is the union  `L = L_horiz ∪ L_vert`. The corner the sofa
must pivot around is the *reflex* corner at (0,0).

A sofa shape can pass the corner **iff** there is a continuous family of
placements putting it inside one corridor copy after the other. Because
translations of the sofa inside the starting corridor can be re-phrased as
translations of the hallway, everything is parametrized by one curve:

* Let **x(t), t ∈ [0, π/2]** be the curve traced **in the sofa's frame** by
  the hallway's reflex corner as the motion proceeds — the *rotation path*,
  with x(0) = (0,0).
* At "time" t the occupied hallway region, seen from the sofa, is the copy
  **x(t) + R_t L** (R_t = CCW rotation by t).
* The largest shape that fits through *this particular motion* is the
  intersection of all copies (Romik eq. (8)):

      S_x  =  L_horiz  ∩  ⋂_{0≤t≤π/2} ( x(t) + R_t L )  ∩  (x(π/2) + R_{π/2} L_vert).

The problem becomes: **maximize the area of S_x over all C¹ rotation paths
x**. The boundary of S_x is generated by the two "corner rays" (partitioning
into pieces traced by the corner) and by wall-contact pieces (the dead parts
of the union).

## 4. Gerver's sofa, built from first principles

### 4.1 Contact paths (Romik, Theorem 1)

Write μ_t = (cos t, sin t), ν_t = (−sin t, cos t). The four "functional"
boundary points of the moving hallway (the two inner-wall grips and two
outer-wall pivots) trace curves in the sofa frame:

    A(t) = x + <x',μ>_t ν + μ          (outer wall, right/front)
    B(t) = x + <x',μ>_t ν              (inner wall, μ-side)
    C(t) = x − <x',ν>_t μ + ν          (outer wall, back/top)
    D(t) = x − <x',ν>_t μ              (inner wall, ν-side)

Implemented and verified in `contact()`. Their endpoints are exact:

    A(0) = (1, 0),   C(0) − D(0) = (0, 1)   (vertical alignment of the two
    back corners C(0), D(0) — x = −1.4206… — not a straight boundary edge),
    B(π/2) = (0.1931…, 0),  C(π/2) = (−2.2275…, 0).

### 4.2 The five ODE phases

Area-maximality forces, on intervals of the boundary, a balanced rotation
condition (Gerver's "balanced" condition; Romik derives the corresponding
differential equations systematically). For the optimal sofa the
rotation path must be exactly C¹ and piecewise-composed of **five** types of
solution SOL1–SOL5 (Romik Theorem 3), switching at

    0 → φ → θ → π/2−θ → π/2−φ → π/2 ,     φ ≈ 0.039177,  θ ≈ 0.681302.

With J = [[0,−1],[1,0]] the path readout on each phase is x(t) = R_t p_j(t) + κ_j with
the SOL pieces (`_P` in the code):

    SOL1: p₁ = (A₁cos t + A₂sin t − 1,   −A₂cos t + A₁sin t − ½)      on [0, φ]
    SOL2: p₂ = (−t²/4 + B₁t + B₂,        t/2 − B₁ − 1)               on [φ, θ]
    SOL3: p₃ = (C₁ − t,                  C₂ + t)                     on [θ, π/2−θ]   (straight line!)
    SOL4: p₄ = (−t/2 + D₁ − 1,           −t²/4 + D₁t + D₂)           on [π/2−θ, π/2−φ]
    SOL5: p₅ = (E₁cos t + E₂sin t − ½,   −E₂cos t + E₁sin t − 1)     on [π/2−φ, π/2]

with x′ = R_t(p′ + Jp), x″ = R_t(2Jp′ + p″ − p). Note the middle phase (3)
makes x move along a **straight line** — the sofa pivots about a fixed
corner contact around t = π/4.

### 4.3 The 22 equations

The construction is pinned by 22 scalar conditions on 22 unknowns
(A₁, A₂, B₁, B₂, C₁, C₂, D₁, D₂, E₁, E₂, φ, θ, and the five κ vectors),
equivalently by the 4×4 system of MathWorld eqs. (1)–(4):

  1. **endpoints** (Romik (23), (26)): A(0) = (1,0); x′(π/2) = −x′(0);
  2. **symmetry** relations e₁ = a₁, e₂ = −a₂, d₁ = π/4 − b₁,
     d₂ = b₂ + (π/4)(2b₁ − π/4), c₂ = c₁ − π/2, k₁₁ = 1 − a₁, k₁₂ = ¼,
     a₂ = −¼ and k₄₂ = k₂₂ (Romik eqs. (27)–(34));
  3. **gluing/hand-offs** (Romik eqs. (43)–(44)):
     x(φ) = B(π/2 − θ)   and   x(π/2 − φ) = D(θ);
  4. **joining C²** (matching x, x′ at the phase edges).

In `gerver_constants()` we re-solve the 4×4 system from scratch by Newton's
method; the Table-1 constants are recovered with residual 2.2e−16.

### 4.5 The shape

The sofa sits in the box `x ∈ [−2.2275…, 1] × [0,1]`, and its CCW
boundary loop is (`sofa_polygon()` in the code):

    A(0) = (1,0)
      → A(t), t: φ → π/2        right outer curve, up to A(π/2) = (0.1931…, 1)
      → flat top edge y = 1     left to C(0) = (−1.4206…, 1)
      → C(t), t: 0 → π/2−φ      C-swoosh down the left flank to C(π/2) = (−2.2275…, 0)
                                (bulges left:  x = −2.108… at y = ½ — NOT a flat back)
      → flat bottom edge y = 0  right to D(0) = (−1.4206…, 0)
      → D(t), t: 0 → θ          funnel curve up to D(θ) = (−1.2246…, 0.05519)
      → x(t), t: π/2−φ → φ      the NICHE CEILING, arcing right to x(φ) = (−0.00293, 0.05519)
      → B(t), t: π/2−θ → π/2    funnel curve down to B(π/2) = (0.1931…, 0)
      → flat bottom edge y = 0  right, closing at (1,0)

So the sofa consists of five smooth boundary curves (A, C, D, the
x-ceiling, B) plus three flat edges (the top strip y = 1 and the two
y = 0 floor strips); Baek's abstract describes the construction as
"Gerver's construction with 18 curve sections". It is a bulky body
whose signature feature is a **deep dome bite carved into the underside,
open to the floor between D(0) = (−1.4206, 0) and B(π/2) = (0.1931, 0)**:
the ceiling x(t), t ∈ [φ, π/2−φ] starts low (height 0.05519 at both ends)
but bulges *upward* to its apex x(π/4) = (−0.6138, 0.6643) — a wide cavity
covering most of the sofa's footprint. The two short curves D and B are
funnels that flare from the ceiling ends down to the floor, and the "flat
back" one might expect from C(0), D(0) being vertically aligned is in fact
the C-swoosh, which bows out to x = −2.108 at mid-height.

That cavity is what lets the corner wall pass through: at the critical
45° pose the inner hallway walls squeeze through the dome with a pinch
clearance of only **0.0012**. This is Gerver's own observation from his
closing remark (1992), recast as Baek's Theorem 8.4.1 / Remark 8.4.1:
the niche 'core' endpoints x(φ), x(π/2−φ) come within 0.0012 of the inner
walls b…(t), d…(t) at t = π/4 — the construction comes within 0.0012 of
'breaking'. We reproduce the value numerically as **0.001238** on each
side (`verification_report.json` [V5]).

### 4.6 The area, exactly

Gerver's analytic computation (as catalogued in MathWorld "Gerver Sofa",
eqs. (1)–(17)) reduces the area to the integral of the piecewise "radius
function" r(α) (with a jump at α = π/2−θ) plus two parabolic-cusp pieces
y₁, y₂:

    S = S_0 + S_1 + S_2,     S_0 = ∫ r(α) dα   (eq. (8) of MathWorld's page),

with the other terms given in closed form (MathWorld eqs. (9)–(17)). In
`gerver_area()` we implement it with **exact closed-form antiderivatives**
on every interval, including the jump of r at π/2−θ — this yields

    S  =  2.219531668871967…  (OEIS A128463: 2.21953166887196…)

with residual +7.6e−15 at double precision, and +7.4e−15 when re-computed
independently in `mp_area_check.py` at 40 decimal digits:
2.21953166887196742554628.

## 6. Why this sofa is optimal (Baek, 2024 — outline)

Baek proves [arXiv:2411.19826, Theorem 1.1.1] that the maximum of the
moving-sofa problem equals Gerver's constant. The proof is organized in
**three steps** (paper §1.2–§1.8 = Chapters 2–8); here is the logic in
broad strokes — the paper contains all precise statements:

1. **Step 1 — reduce the possible shapes of a maximum sofa S_max.**
   * *Monotone (Ch. 2).* Working with the supporting hallways of S, the
     intersection ℐ(S) of all hallway copies is connected (Theorem 2.3.6),
     contains S, and can itself move through the corner — and taking the
     intersection again does not enlarge it (Theorem 2.4.4). So S_max may
     be assumed **monotone**, i.e. S = ℐ(S): a convex body with a dent —
     the **niche** — carved out by the inner corner of the supporting
     hallways. Writing S = cap K ∖ niche N(K), the area becomes
     |K| − |N(K)|, and the problem is reposed as maximizing over caps.
   * *Balanced (Ch. 3).* Gerver's balancing argument (parallel supporting
     lines one unit apart must carry equal total edge length) shows the
     maximum polygons converge to a **balanced maximum cap**
     (Theorems 3.4.9/3.4.10). Gerver's original Theorem 1 had a logical
     gap — it never established connectedness of the approximating
     polygons or of the limit; Baek reworks the proof rigorously.
   * *Full rotation (Ch. 4).* The rotation angle ω of a maximum sofa
     equals π/2 (Theorem 1.5.2): the triangular region Δ_ω is disjoint
     from S_ω, which leaves room to rotate the sofa by the extra angle
     π/2 − ω inside the horizontal corridor before performing the motion.
2. **Step 2 — the injectivity condition (Ch. 6).** The trajectory of the
   inner corner (0,0), seen in the sofa's frame (our x(t)), must not make
   self-loops. This is established by a **new differential inequality**
   on S_max (Eq. (1.9) of the paper), heavily inspired by Romik's
   balancing ODE for the differential sides of Gerver's sofa. Injectivity
   is the key hypothesis that makes the upper bound of Step 3 rigorous.
3. **Step 3 — the upper-bound functional 𝒬 and concavity (Ch. 8).**
   * *(a)* The class of sofas with the injectivity condition is extended
     to a convex domain ℒ of triples (K, B, D) of convex bodies — parts
     of the region R enclosing S — into which each S maps one-to-one
     (Brunn–Minkowski theory).
   * *(b)* A **quadratic functional 𝒬** on ℒ is built (Green's theorem
     along ∂R, quadratic area expressions from Brunn–Minkowski), and the
     injectivity condition + Jordan curve theorem establish
     **area(S) ≤ 𝒬(K, B, D)**.
   * *(c)* **Mamikon's theorem** ("tangent sweep ≡ tangent cluster")
     shows 𝒬 is **concave** on ℒ.
   * *(d)* The directional derivative of 𝒬 at the triple arising from
     Gerver's sofa is computed to be non-positive, using Romik's local
     optimality ODEs on G — i.e. the five-phase SOL1–SOL5 structure, with
     its numerically determined φ and θ, is precisely a local optimum of
     𝒬. Concavity upgrades local ⇒ global. Since 𝒬(G) = area(G) =
     2.21953166887196…, G maximizes the area. ∎

A pleasing detail: the upper bound is **tight with no slack**, and it is
nontrivially so — the construction comes within 0.0012 of *breaking*
(the pinch of §4.5), which is why the clearance computation is not a
curiosity but a load-bearing part of the landscape.

## 7. What remains open (variants)

* **Ambidextrous sofa** (left *and* right turns): best known lower bound
  1.64495 (Romik's piecewise-algebraic sofa); upper bound = 2.37.
* **General turns** (angle ≠ 90°), two consecutive corners, trapezoid rooms:
  active numerical territory; Georgiev–Gómez-Serrano–Tao–Wagner studied
  discovery-at-scale variants (2025).
* **The 3-D sofa** (turning a mattress in a 3-D corner): essentially open.
* A rigorous *uniqueness* statement for the mover x(t) beyond Baek's class.
